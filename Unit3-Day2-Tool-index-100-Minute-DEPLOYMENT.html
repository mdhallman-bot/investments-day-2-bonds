3. What Is a Bond? — Build the Mechanics

Direct Instruction + Notebook
Tier 3 preview — words you will use in this section
BondA loan made by an investor to an issuer.
IssuerThe borrower that sells the bond.
Principal / Face ValueThe amount borrowed and scheduled to be repaid.
Coupon RateThe stated annual interest rate.
MaturityThe date the principal is scheduled to be repaid.
U.S. Treasury NoteDebt issued by the U.S. Treasury; a 10-year note matures in 10 years.
Core idea: When you buy a bond, you are lending money to an issuer. In return, the issuer promises to make agreed-upon interest payments and return the bond's principal (face value) at maturity.
PAUSE FOR DIRECT INSTRUCTION.
Use the board/slides to walk through one bond from beginning to end: Investor → lends principal → Issuer → pays interest → returns principal at maturity. Students should not move on until the class has traced who gives money to whom, who owes whom, and what happens at maturity.

Investor

The person or organization that buys the bond and lends the money.

Issuer

The government, company, or other organization borrowing the money.

Principal / Face Value

The amount borrowed that is promised back at maturity.

Coupon Rate

The stated annual interest rate used to determine interest payments.

Maturity

The date when the issuer is scheduled to repay the principal.

Trace the Money

When an investor buys a newly issued bond, who is borrowing the money?
What does the issuer promise to return at maturity?
What is the investor receiving in exchange for lending money?

Put the Vocabulary in Your Own Words

Core distinction: A stock is ownership. A bond is lending.

Simple Bond Calculation

You buy a $1,000 bond with a 5% annual coupon rate. Assume it pays that amount once per year and you hold it for 4 years.

U.S. Treasury Securities — Government Borrowing

PAUSE FOR DIRECT INSTRUCTION.
Define a 10-year U.S. Treasury note: a debt security issued by the U.S. Department of the Treasury that borrows money from investors for 10 years and pays interest along the way before returning the face value at maturity.
Why investors care: U.S. Treasuries are commonly treated as having very low default risk because they are backed by the U.S. government's ability to tax and borrow. They are not risk-free: their purchasing power can be hurt by inflation, and their market value can change when interest rates change.
Open the U.S. Debt Clock and look at the current national debt:

https://www.usdebtclock.org/

Question: When you see that enormous debt number, where did all that borrowed money come from?
Speaking frames for the class discussion:
“I think some of the money came from ___ because ___.”
“When someone buys a Treasury security, the investor ___ and the U.S. government ___.”
DIRECT-INSTRUCTION CONNECTION: Much of federal borrowing happens by selling Treasury securities. Buyers can include individuals, banks, investment funds, pension funds, foreign investors and governments, the Federal Reserve, and government accounts that hold Treasury securities. The debt represents accumulated borrowing over time, not one single loan.

5. Bond Risks — “Safer” Does Not Mean “No Risk”

Substantial Risk Section · Scenarios
Tier 3 preview — bond risks
Default / Credit RiskThe issuer might not make promised payments.
Interest-Rate RiskChanging market rates can make an existing bond less attractive.
Inflation RiskFixed payments may lose purchasing power as prices rise.
Liquidity RiskThe bond may be difficult to sell quickly at a fair price.
Bonds are often described as less risky than stocks, but different bonds face different risks. Your job is to identify what could go wrong for the investor.

Default / Credit Risk

The issuer may fail to make promised interest or principal payments.

Interest-Rate Risk

When new market rates rise, an existing lower-rate bond becomes less attractive.

Inflation Risk

Fixed dollar payments may buy less in the future if prices rise.

Liquidity Risk

You may have difficulty selling a bond quickly at a price you consider fair.

Sort

A. A company that issued your bond runs out of cash and misses an interest payment.
B. Your bond pays 3%, but new similar bonds begin paying 6%.
C. You keep receiving the promised fixed payments, but prices for food, rent, and transportation rise quickly.
D. You need cash immediately, but very few buyers are interested in your particular bond.

Two Bonds

Bond A: 10-year U.S. Treasury note paying 4.2%
Bond B: 10-year bond from a financially shaky company paying 8.5%

Both promise interest and repayment of principal. Why might Bond B need to offer a much higher rate?
Important: A higher interest rate is not automatically “better.” It may be for taking more risk.

9. Naming What You Found — Portfolio & Diversification

After Class Debrief
Tier 3 preview — portfolio thinking
PortfolioAll of the investments a person or organization owns together.
DiversificationSpreading investments across different holdings or asset types.
Risk & ReturnRisk is the chance of loss; return is the gain or loss on an investment.
Important: Diversification can reduce risk. It does not guarantee that a portfolio cannot lose money.
